The Wealth Tax: Creating a Fairer, Greener Economy - Green Party Skip to content Skip to footer The Wealth Tax: Creating a Fairer, Greener Economy 14 September 2026 / 15 September 2026 by Green Party By Marcus Thornley, member of the Green World Editorial Board In recent years, the conversation around the UK economy has shifted. As the gap between stagnant wages and soaring asset values continues to grow, economists and policymakers are looking at ways to make tax fairer – to ensure that our tax system reflects how wealth is actually generated in the 21st century. Most people in the UK pay tax on their income (e.g. salary) and on consumption (e.g. VAT). But the wealthier you are, and the more things you own, the more likely that your income comes from owning (assets), rather than earning (working a job). And at the moment, the owners usually pay a lower tax rate than the earners. The Green Party is looking at ways to fix this and to make tax fair across income and assets. One way is a Wealth Tax. This is a way to rebalance the scales and ensure that those who have benefited the most from society contribute a small, fair portion back into the foundations that made their success possible. What is a Wealth Tax? At its core, a Wealth Tax is a modest annual charge on the total value of assets – such as property, stocks, and luxury investments – owned by the very wealthiest individuals. These assets don’t just stay static, they generate wealth themselves and often grow far faster than salaries or savings. The Green Party’s proposal is highly targeted. It wouldn’t affect the homes, pensions, or savings of over 99.9% of the population. Instead, it applies only to those with assets worth more than £10 million. For the 0.04% at this level , a contribution of just 1% (or 2% for billionaires) would be a marginal amount, but it would be life-changing for our country. The tax on assets and other elements of Green Party tax proposals generate over £30 billion a year: Reforming Capital Gains Tax to align the rates paid by taxpayers on income and taxable gains Aligning tax rates on investment income with the tax and National Insurance Contribution rates on employment income Put together, these proposals offer real hope for a fairer system that rewards hard work not wealth. The Argument for Fairness The current system taxes “work” more than “wealth” – “earning” more than “owning”. While your salary is taxed directly, wealth assets often grow tax-free. For instance, a main home, however big, is exempt from Capital Gains Tax, and stocks only trigger tax when sold – often at lower rates than income. This creates a fundamental unfairness: those who live off existing assets pay a much smaller share of their “gains” than those who work for a living, allowing extreme wealth to compound while wages are squeezed. A wealth tax tackles this fundamental unfairness – by addressing the growing gap between workers and the mega-wealthy and acknowledging the economic contribution wider society makes towards an individual’s wealth: The growing gap: Under the current system, a nurse or a teacher pays a higher effective tax rate than a billionaire whose wealth increases by millions through rising property or stock market values. Economic Contribution: A wealth tax acknowledges that extreme fortunes are rarely built in a vacuum; they rely on public infrastructure, an educated workforce, and a stable legal system. Think of this small annual contribution as a “user fee” for the societal stability that allows such wealth to be made. Addressing Common Concerns Implementing a wealth tax is a bold move and is often met with specific concerns. Let’s look at the most common critiques with a level head: “Won’t the super-rich simply leave?” The “exodus” argument is a common concern. However, studies of similar taxes often show that “tax-induced migration” is lower than anticipated. Wealth is frequently tied to local networks, specific business environments, and high-value real estate that cannot be moved. Non-dom tax changes offer an insight into how this might work in practice. Favorable tax status was removed for non-doms in April 2025, but it led to less than 1% of non-doms leaving the country. “What if someone is asset rich but cash poor?” This is a valid technical challenge, but at a £10 million threshold it is unlikely that individuals with over £10 million in assets would face genuine hardship by reducing their wealth to pay this tax. “Is it fair to tax money twice?” We already pay multiple layers of tax – for example, when you go shopping with post-tax income and pay VAT on the purchase. More importantly, much of the wealth at the top tier comes from the rising value of assets (capital gains) rather than just saved wages. A wealth tax ensures that this “unearned” growth (the growth while you sleep) helps fund the society that made the growth possible. How This Benefits the Many A wealth tax isn’t just about the numbers for the top 0.1%; it’s about the quality of life for everyone. By raising an estimated £15 billion or more every year, we can invest in the things that matter: Lowering Energy Bills: Funding a nationwide programme to insulate homes, making them warmer and cheaper to run. Lowering Water Bills: Taking water companies into public control, lowering bills and cleaning up our rivers and seas while we’re at it Tackling the cost of living: Providing free school meals to all primary and secondary school children to help with inflation of the price of food A Positive Way Forward A wealth tax isn’t about punishing success – it’s about rebalancing the scales and asking those who have gained the most from our country to contribute their fair share. Along with other measures like equalising income and capital gains tax rates, it modernises our tax system and ensures that as the country grows, nobody is left behind. By asking a tiny fraction of the population to contribute a little more, we can build a Britain that is richer, more stable, and demonstrably fairer for everyone.
