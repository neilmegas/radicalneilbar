Has football lost its soul? - Green Party Skip to content Skip to footer Has football lost its soul? 22 September 2026 / 24 September 2026 by Green Party By Toby Stephenson, freelance journalist While the hope (and pain) of England and Scotland’s World Cup exits may be receding, the controversy over Gianni Infantino’s plan to sell off a stake in the world’s premier football competition kicks on. At the end of August, Uefa began preparing criminal legal proceedings against Infantino and the FA have called for the release of all documents relating to the aborted proposal. So with the summer behind us, what can this sorry saga, of events off the pitch and in closed boardrooms, tell us about the state of the beautiful game? The World Cup When President Donald Trump rang Fifa president Gianni Infantino to ask him to take a second look at the red card given to American star striker Folarin Balogun, most assumed it would come to nothing; a typical Trumpian tantrum that would soon be forgotten. Instead, Fifa suspended the ban and Balogun was back on the pitch for his next game. Incredibly, of the eighteen members of Fifa’s own Disciplinary Committee, seventeen were never even consulted. One man made the call, both literally and figuratively. In football terms, it turned out to be a small moment. A single yellow-red decision that had no real impact on the tournament. However, it said something far bigger about who football answers to in 2026. Seventy-two members of the European Parliament have since written demanding an investigation into whether US pressure shaped Fifa’s decision. The game’s own ethics watchdogs are being asked to examine the man in charge of running it. And all of this comes just months after Infantino handed Trump a prize for promoting world peace; a decision that was comically ironic at best, and morally deplorable at worst. Yet none of this happened in a vacuum. This is the same Fifa that awarded the 2034 World Cup to Saudi Arabia without a genuine rival bid, and the same organisation that pressed ahead with a US-hosted tournament whilst a travel ban kept a match official and fans from a quarter of qualifying nations from making it through customs. Then more recently Infantino was at it again, unveiling plans to sell a stake in Fifa to a group of private investors, including a firm with close ties to the Trump family. Fortunately, the backlash against the Fifa Forward Enterprise (FFE) proposal was immediate and furious, with Fifa forced to back away from this plan for now. However, what won’t have shifted is the pattern. Infantino has built a habit of making himself useful to the powerful, almost always to the detriment of the game. This is a pattern worth pausing on, because it’s a story that transcends football altogether. Strip away the offside rule and the transfer fees, and what’s left is something uncomfortably familiar; a small number of very wealthy people controlling something that matters to millions of ordinary lives, with little regard for what those millions actually want. It’s the same imbalance that shows up in who owns the energy grid, or who decides what gets built on the local playing field. Football just happens to make it easy to see, because it’s a game we all grew up with, played by rules everyone understands, and watched by people who have never had a vote in how it’s run. This is why a different way of thinking about the game matters, and, more importantly, this is why Green politics matters. The Story at Home It’s easy to see this summer’s World Cup saga as a uniquely American intrusion into football. But look closer to home, at the clubs fans have followed for generations, and an uncannily similar picture emerges. Really, it comes down to one simple question: who are these clubs run for now? For many supporters, the answer is that it is no longer for them. Few clubs embody this as clearly as Manchester United. This is a club founded by workers from the Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway, its fanbase’s roots running through those humble, grassroots beginnings. Today, that history sits uneasily alongside a boardroom shaped not by Manchester, but by American finance, British industry, and an imperative to maximise profits. The shift began in 2005, when Malcolm Glazer completed a leveraged buyout of the club, loading the debt used to buy United onto the club itself. Fans woke up supporting a team that now owed hundreds of millions of pounds, essentially for the privilege of being bought. The response was immediate and remarkably unified. Green and gold, United’s original colours before red became synonymous with the club, became a symbol of protest, worn on scarves by supporters demanding the Glazers leave. However, two decades on, and that protest has never really gone away. By 2023, with the club haemorrhaging money, the Glazers finally opened the door to new investment. For a while, it looked like United might change hands entirely, with a Qatari-backed consortium making a serious, sustained bid to buy the club outright. For many fans, almost any alternative to the Glazers, any alternative to the owners that they felt shifted the club away from the fans, felt like progress. In the end however, the Glazers chose a different route. Rather than selling up, they brought in British billionaire Sir Jim Ratcliffe, whose petrochemicals firm INEOS took a 27.7% stake and full control of football operations. Not only did the Glazers remain, a new billionaire joined them, intent on making Manchester United “the most profitable club in the world”. What followed was a wave of cost-cutting; hundreds of jobs lost at the club’s Carrington training complex, concessionary ticket prices for children and over-65s scrapped, and season ticket prices raised for four years running. Fans who had spent two decades protesting debt-driven, profit-maximising decision-making now found themselves funding a different billionaire’s business venture. Then, just this June, INEOS Limited restructured its shareholding to hold beneficial control of 54.54% of United’s Class A shares, just as the club’s net debt passed $1 billion for the first time in its history. As the Manchester United Supporters’ Trust put it bluntly when concessions were axed, the idea that fans should pay their “fair share” for the club’s “excesses and/or mismanagement” after two decades of Glazer-era debt is downright offensive. This is by design, not necessity Crucially, none of this is inevitable. It’s easy to talk about football finance as if it obeys its own natural laws; debt piles up, fans pay more, clubs drift further from the communities that built them, and that’s just modern football. But other models prove otherwise. In Germany, the Bundesliga’s ’50+1′ rule keeps nearly all clubs majority-owned by their own members, blocking outside investors from taking control. Choices can be made differently, by different people, answering to different priorities. It’s the same instinct behind Green Party policy on football. Rather than treating clubs as commodities to be bought and sold, the Greens have proposed a £5bn package to properly fund grassroots and community sport. This sits alongside the proposal for a new mechanism letting local authorities buy shares in the professional clubs based in their own area, with any dividends reinvested straight back into local pitches and coaching, not a shareholder’s pocket. It matters because, as a recent GMB study found, nearly a thousand public football pitches have been lost across Britain since 2010. This is the same drift away from local ownership, just playing out on a smaller, more intimate scale. However, strip away the policy detail and what’s left is a simple conviction: football should belong to the people who make it what it is, not to whoever can afford to buy it. A club isn’t just a balance sheet with a badge on it. It’s the Saturday routine passed from parent to child, the shared identity of a town or a city, a source of meaning that has nothing to do with annual profits. When ownership treats a club as an asset to be leveraged, refinanced, and eventually sold on, something gets lost that no amount of Champions League football can buy back. None of this requires romanticising the past, or pretending English football was some utopia before money took over. Clubs have always needed investment to compete. But there’s a meaningful difference between a club that needs investment and a club that’s been turned into one. At its core, football is, and always has been, a game shaped by the people who love it, rather than the people who own it. Conclusion So, has football lost its soul? Watching a president’s phone call bend a red card decision, or watching a club built by railway workers carry a billion pounds of debt under its third owner in twenty years, it would be easy to say yes, and leave it there. But the game continues to refuse this identification. Fans still turn up in their thousands, chanting the same songs they have for generations. Kids still grow up wishing to make their local team great again. The green and gold scarves still appear at Old Trafford, two decades after the Glazers arrived. The Blackpool fans who boycotted their own ground for four years got their regulator built, even if the argument over where it sits continues. The Manchester City supporters campaigning for lower season-ticket prices got them. None of these are small things. They’re proof that the soul of the game hasn’t disappeared, it’s just been pushed to the margins, still fighting for a voice against the money and the power that currently hold the microphone. That’s really what a Green World means for football; not a return to some imagined golden age, but a shift in whose voice counts. A game where a red card is decided by the eighteen people whose job it is to decide it, not the one phone call that gets answered. A club where fans have a genuine stake, not just a season ticket and a shirt. A sport, in other words, that answers to the people who turn up every week, rather than the people who choose not to. Trump got his phone call answered. Infantino maintains his power for now. The question this summer’s World Cup has left behind is who will answer for the rest of us.
